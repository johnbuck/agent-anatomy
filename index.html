Agent Anatomy

Meet the agent

An AI agent isn't one thing — it's a body of parts wired together. Below: each piece, mapped onto a body you already understand. The rest of this guide takes them one tab at a time.

Each tab in this guide unpacks one part of the body — the brain (Models), what fits in mind right now (Context), the body around it (Harness), the library it can consult (RAG), the journal it keeps (Memory), the recipe cards in the backpack (Skills), the gear bolted on (Plugins & MCP), and where the data goes (Data). Models

A "model" is the brain. Different brains have different sizes, speeds, and costs. You pick the right brain for the job.

Where the brains come from

Above is one company's lineup. There are several major sources — each with its own flavor.

Hosted by Anthropic Hosted by OpenAI Self-hosted on your hardware Router / aggregator
Same family, different sizes

Pick the right brain

Don't use a brain surgeon to read your mail. A small model answers quickly and cheaply; a large one reasons through hard problems. Most workflows mix both.

Many makers

Not just one company

Anthropic (Claude), OpenAI (GPT), Google (Gemini), Meta (Llama), and local open-source models all exist. They behave differently — like authors with different writing styles.

Multimodal

Words aren't all

Modern models also see images, hear audio, and read documents. The brain is the same; it just has more senses wired in.

Thinking — models that reason before they answer

Some models "think out loud" before responding. They generate a private chain of reasoning — sometimes visible to you, sometimes hidden — working through the problem step by step, then write the final answer. Slower and more expensive, but much better at hard problems.

When it pays off

Hard problems with chained steps

Reasoning models are worth the cost on problems where multiple steps have to chain together cleanly: complex math, multi-hop logic, code that needs to handle edge cases, planning under constraints. On simple Q&A or short content, they're slower and pricier for no real win.

The price

5–10× slower, 5–20× the bill

Reasoning models run noticeably slower — sometimes minutes per response — and the provider bills for the invisible thinking tokens too, often 5–20× the visible output. A reasoning call on a hard problem can land at $5–50 per request.

Real examples

Same trick, different flavors

OpenAI's o-series (o3, o4) emit thinking before answering. Anthropic's Claude has extended thinking mode. Google's Gemini has deep-thinking modes. All share the same trick: spend more tokens thinking, get a more accurate answer.

The takeaway

A model is rented intelligence. You pay per word in and per word out. Bigger model = smarter, slower, more expensive. Reasoning models add a second dial: spend more on thinking, get a better answer. Smart product design uses the cheapest model that gets the job right — and reaches for reasoning only when the problem needs it.

Tokens

A token is a chunk of text — usually a whole word, sometimes a piece of one. It's the unit of measurement for almost everything that matters: how fast a model is, how much fits in the context window, and what each request costs.

The unit

Words, usually — sometimes pieces

Common words map to one token each ("the" → 1). Rare or compound words split into pieces: "tokenization" → 2 tokens, "antidisestablishmentarianism" → 7. Punctuation and spaces count too.

Two sides of the bill

Tokens in, tokens out

Every request has two prices: tokens in (what you send the model) and tokens out (what it writes back). Output is typically 3–5× more expensive than input — generating is harder than reading.

Scale check

1 million tokens ≈ 1,500 pages

A long memo: ~1K tokens. A novel chapter: 5–10K. A whole paperback: ~100K. An encyclopedia: 10M+. When a model advertises "200K context," that's roughly 300 pages it can hold at once.

What it costs

Prices vary wildly — even within one provider. Below: per-million-token pricing for representative models, with concrete examples of what it costs to push a 300-page book through each.

Tier matters more than maker

Pick the right tier first

Within any provider, the small tier is 10–50× cheaper than the flagship. Most production workflows split jobs across tiers — small models for routing, classification, and easy work; big models only for the hard reasoning. Most of the cost lives in routing decisions, not model choice.

Where the bill lands

Output dominates the cost

Apps usually send much more input than they get back — but because each output token costs 3–5× more, the bill often splits closer to 50/50. Worth optimizing both: trim what you send, and tell the model to be concise.

Caching — paying less when nothing changes

Reading a long prompt is expensive and slow. So providers cache the work they did on the steady parts — your system prompt, early conversation, long reference documents — and charge a fraction of the usual price when those parts are unchanged on the next call. You still pay; you just pay much less. The catch: caches don't last forever, and different providers run different stopwatches.

What gets cached

Only the prefix

Just the stable run of text at the start of your prompt — system prompt, early conversation, reference documents. The model's reply, your next question, and anything that changes flows fresh through every time.

Why it matters

Cheaper and faster

A cache hit costs roughly 10–50% of a cache miss and starts streaming back in a fraction of the latency. On long contexts — say a 100-page document the agent keeps consulting — the savings change what's economically possible.

Where it goes wrong: if your harness reshuffles tools or memories at the start of each turn, you blow the cache every time and pay full price. Stable order is everything.

What kills it

Time, or any edit to the prefix

Two things invalidate a cache: the clock running out (the bar above), or any change to the cached text. Adding one word to your system prompt forces a full re-read from byte one.

The takeaway

Tokens are the unit of currency in AI. Everything you care about — speed, cost, what fits in the context window — is measured in them. The cheapest performance win in most agents isn't paying for a smarter model; it's sending fewer tokens, and keeping the prefix steady so caching can work.

Context

A model has no memory of yesterday. Every time you talk to it, you hand it a stack of paper called "context." That stack has a maximum size — and reading a thick stack gets sloppy near the middle.

Context rot performance degrades as the context fills up

Below ~50% fullness the model stays sharp. Cross that line and the drop is steep — by 70% you're getting noticeably slower, less reliable answers, and near 100% the model starts missing things outright. This is why "just dump everything in" stops working.

Compaction how agents handle long conversations

When the stack gets too full, the agent collapses old chunks into short summaries. You lose some detail, but the conversation can keep going indefinitely.

The takeaway

The model is a goldfish that re-reads its whole notebook every time you speak. Bigger notebook = slower, more expensive, and easier to lose your place. Good agents are careful about what they keep in the notebook.

Harness

A bare model only takes text in and puts text out. The harness is the scaffolding around the model — the part that holds the conversation, runs tools on the model's behalf, asks for permission, and decides what the model gets to see on every turn. It's the difference between a brain in a jar and something you can actually drive.

It runs the loop

The model speaks once per turn

The model doesn't "stay running." Every turn, the harness packages a fresh prompt, sends it, reads the reply, and decides whether to loop again. Without a harness, there's no agent — just one isolated reply.

It runs the tools

The model can't press buttons

When the model says "please edit this file" or "please search the web," it's writing a request — the harness is what actually does it, after checking with you if needed. Tools live in the harness, not in the model.

It curates the view

It decides what the model sees

Every turn, the harness picks which memories to load, which files to include, how much history to keep, and where to spend the context budget. Different harnesses make different choices — that's most of why they feel different.

What "assemble" actually means

Every turn, the harness pulls each of these pieces from its own source and stacks them into a single prompt before handing it to the model. This is the same context window we saw in the previous tab — now from the harness's side: it's the harness's job to build this on every turn.

Subagents — when the agent sends a helper

Sometimes a job is too big or too messy to do in one head. A capable harness lets the main agent spawn a smaller helper — a subagent — to handle a piece of the work in its own private context, then return just a short summary. The mess stays downstairs.

Context hygiene

The mess stays downstairs

Whatever the subagent reads — 200 files, a wall of search results, a long log — lives only in its context window. The main agent gets back a clean summary and never has to wade through the raw material. When the subagent finishes, that context is thrown away.

Parallel work

Many heads at once

Independent tasks can run as separate subagents at the same time. "Search these five places in parallel" finishes in roughly the time of one search, not five — the harness juggles them and collects the summaries.

Specialization

Different helpers, different jobs

A subagent can have its own instructions and a narrower toolset — a researcher, a code reviewer, a verifier. The main agent picks the right specialist for each piece of work, like a manager handing tasks to people with sharper focus.

Real harnesses, side by side

The same model behaves very differently depending on which harness is wrapped around it.

Claude Code

Terminal-first, code-shaped

Anthropic's CLI harness. Lives in your terminal, edits files, runs commands, asks permission for risky operations, manages git. Built for shipping code. This explainer was built with it.

Cursor · Windsurf · Continue

Bolted into the editor

Harnesses that live inside a code editor. Same model under the hood, but the harness sees your open files, your cursor, your project structure — and slots its suggestions directly into the editor.

ChatGPT · Claude.ai

Chat-app harnesses

The web and mobile apps. Tuned for conversation: rich formatting, images, file uploads, voice. Fewer raw "do things in the world" tools than developer harnesses — by design.

OpenClaw · custom builds

Roll your own

Open-source harnesses you can self-host, or fully custom ones built on a vendor SDK. More control and privacy, but every feature — permissions, memory, hooks, tools — is yours to design and maintain.

The takeaway

A model on its own is a brain in a jar — it can write but it can't do. The harness gives it hands, eyes, a steering wheel, and a sense of where it's been. The same model in two different harnesses is, practically speaking, two different products.

RAG

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Before answering, the agent searches a pile of your private documents for the most relevant chunks, then hands those chunks to the model along with your question. It's how an agent stays grounded in information it wasn't trained on — without dumping a whole knowledge base into context every time.

The win

Search by meaning, not keywords

Vector search finds chunks that are about the same thing, not chunks that share words. "How do I get my money back?" matches "refund policy" even though no word is shared. Old-school keyword search would miss this entirely.

Why it scales

Only what's needed

You might have 10,000 documents. RAG sends maybe 5 short chunks per question. The model never sees the rest. The knowledge base can grow without growing the context budget — the opposite of "just paste it all in."

Why it's flexible

Fresh, not frozen

The vector database is yours — update it any time. New policy doc? Re-index it and the agent answers using the new version the next minute. The model itself never changes; what it reads changes. No retraining required.

What's a vector database?

A vector database stores the "fingerprints" of your documents. Each chunk gets converted to a list of numbers (an embedding) that captures its meaning. Two chunks about the same topic have similar fingerprints — even if they share no words. The database is built to find nearest neighbors fast, across millions of fingerprints.

Fingerprints

Embeddings capture meaning

Each chunk of text gets turned into a list of ~1,000 numbers. The numbers themselves are nonsense to humans, but they place the chunk in a "semantic space" where similar things sit close together. This is what makes meaning-search possible.

One job, very fast

Built for similarity lookup

A vector database stores millions of these fingerprints and finds nearest neighbors in milliseconds — even across a corpus too big to scan linearly. That's the one thing it's optimized for.

Many flavors

Managed or DIY

Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Chroma — managed services with their own clouds. Postgres + pgvector — DIY on infrastructure you already run. Choice usually comes down to managed convenience vs hosting it yourself for privacy or cost.

The takeaway

RAG turns "the model doesn't know our stuff" into "the model can read up before answering." It's the standard pattern for grounding agents in private data, and it scales: the knowledge base can grow without growing the context window. The model never learns your data — it just gets handed the right pages right before it speaks.

Memory

If the model is a goldfish, memory is the notebook it consults before every conversation. AGENTS.md / CLAUDE.md are the house rules pinned to the wall.

Memory (per user)

Travels with you

Notes the agent writes about you: preferences, who you are, things you've asked it to remember. Persists across every conversation. Lives in your account, not the project.

Where it goes wrong: retrieval is fuzzy — the agent picks the note that sounds closest to your question, not necessarily the right one. Stale notes don't expire on their own. And every loaded note eats context budget — past a few dozen, the truly useful ones get harder to spot.

AGENTS.md (per project)

Travels with the codebase

A markdown file checked into the repo. Anyone who works on that project — human or agent — gets the same house rules: how to deploy, what's forbidden, where things live.

Where it goes wrong: rules accumulate without pruning, leading to contradictions ("always X" written one month, "never X" written the next). And every rule rides along on every turn — a wall of rules leaves less room to think, even on tasks they don't apply to.

The takeaway

Memory is what makes an agent feel like it knows you — but every memory is a bet that the fact will stay true and that the agent will retrieve it correctly. Curate sparingly: a small, fresh set beats a sprawling, stale one. Prune like a garden, not a hoarder's attic.

Skills

A skill is a recipe card the agent pulls off the shelf when it sees a job that matches. Skills let an agent do specialized work without bloating every conversation with instructions it doesn't need.

On-demand

Loaded just-in-time

Skills sit on a shelf until they match. Hundreds of skills can exist without slowing the agent down — only the relevant one comes off the shelf.

Where it goes wrong: the matching is keyword-based. A "when to use" that's too vague ("use when working on code") fires for everything; too narrow ("pytest -k on Tuesday") fires for nothing. From your side, both look identical — the skill behaves inconsistently.

Markdown, not code

Anyone can write one

A skill is a plain markdown file with a name, a "when to use" description, and steps. No programming required.

Where it goes wrong: there's no compiler to catch a sloppy "when to use," and no warning if two skills overlap on the same trigger. The agent silently picks one — usually the first — and the others go unused. You only notice when output gets weird.

Shareable

Bottle your expertise

Have a procedure that always works? Write it as a skill once, and every agent on the team uses it the same way every time.

Where it goes wrong: skills age out. A recipe written for last year's tooling still triggers, but the steps inside no longer reflect how the system actually works. The agent confidently runs an obsolete recipe. Prune like any other code.

The takeaway

Skills turn one-off prompts into reusable procedures, but the win comes from a small library of sharp, well-described skills — not a sprawling shelf of half-overlapping ones. The agent can only run a skill if it knows when to reach for it; if it can't tell two apart, you get inconsistency.

Plugins

A plugin is a sealed box that ships tools, hooks, and skills together. Install the box, get all three. Uninstall, and they're gone cleanly.

🔧 Tools

New verbs

Things the agent can do — call APIs, edit files, query a database. Each tool is a function the agent picks up like a new appendage.

🪝 Hooks

Tripwires

Code that fires before or after specific events. "Before any merge, ping the channel." "After every file edit, run the linter." Automatic, not asked for.

📐 Skills

Playbooks

The procedures that combine the tools and the hooks into something useful end-to-end. Plugins ship skills so installing the plugin gives you the workflow, not just raw pieces.

The takeaway

Plugins are how an agent grows. A vanilla agent talks. With plugins, it can file tickets, deploy code, draft emails, query your data warehouse, post to Slack — and it picks up house rules and safety checks along with the new abilities.

MCP Servers

MCP stands for Model Context Protocol — an open standard that lets any agent talk to any service through a single shared plug. Think USB for AI tools. Before USB, every peripheral had its own custom cable; after USB, one socket served everything. MCP is the same bet for AI.

Standard plug

One protocol, any harness

Write the connector once, every MCP-aware agent uses it. A GitHub server works in Claude Code, in Cursor, in your own Python script — same interface, no rewrites per harness.

Three primitives

Tools, resources, and prompts

An MCP server can expose three things: tools (actions to take), resources (data the agent can read), and prompts (ready-made templates a user can pick). Most servers focus on tools, but the protocol covers more.

Runs where you want it

Self-hostable by default

MCP servers are separate processes you start yourself. For sensitive systems — your database, your inbox, your internal wiki — the server can live entirely inside your network. Nothing leaves until you decide it should.

Plugins vs MCP servers — close cousins, not the same

Both extend what an agent can do. They package it differently.

Plugins

Bundles, harness-specific

Tools + hooks + skills packaged together for one specific harness. Runs inside the harness process. Rich: can intercept events, ship workflows, register skills. Not portable — a Claude Code plugin won't run in Cursor or Continue without a rewrite.

MCP servers

Connectors, harness-agnostic

Tools (plus resources and prompts) exposed over a standard wire protocol. Runs as a separate process, talked to by the harness over stdio or HTTP. Narrower scope, but fully portable: the same server plugs into any MCP-aware harness.

The takeaway

MCP is to AI tools what USB became to peripherals: a boring, universal plug. Once a service has an MCP server, every MCP-aware agent — no matter who built it — can talk to it. The boring part is exactly why it works.

Data

Where does your prompt go after you send it? What gets logged? Will your conversation train the next model version? The answers depend on the provider, the plan, and the harness — and "depends" is often the most honest answer.

The training question

"Is my data used to train future models?" is the first question most people ask. The answer depends sharply on how you're accessing the model.

Developer API

Usually opt-out by default

For all major API providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google), API traffic is not used to train future models by default. This is the contractual baseline for paid developer access. Logs may still exist for safety review and abuse detection — but those don't feed back into training.

Consumer chat apps

Often opt-in by default

The free and lower-tier consumer apps (ChatGPT free, Gemini free, etc.) frequently use your conversations to improve future models — unless you go into settings and turn it off. Some paid tiers flip this. Always read the privacy settings before pasting sensitive content.

Enterprise plans

Contractually opt-out

Enterprise and business tiers add legal guarantees: no training, often shorter or zero log retention, sometimes private deployments. This is what regulated industries use. The protection lives in the contract, not the technology.

Self-hosted

N/A — there's no provider

When you run an open-source model on your own hardware, there's no provider hop to worry about. Your prompts never leave your network. But you also take on hardware, performance, and maintenance costs — and most open models lag the flagship hosted ones.

What self-hosting actually buys you

Self-hosting is the loudest privacy answer, but it's not a clean win. Here's what it does and doesn't do.

What it eliminates

The provider hop

Your prompts never reach a third party's servers. No external logging, no risk of training, no ToS to read. For sensitive data (medical, legal, regulated), this is often the only acceptable answer.

What it doesn't eliminate

Everything else

The harness still runs somewhere. The network still carries traffic. The MCP servers your agent talks to are still doing their thing. If any of those are hosted elsewhere, you've only moved the boundary — you haven't closed it.

What it costs

Real money & real work

Hardware (GPUs aren't cheap), electricity, cooling, model updates, performance gap vs hosted flagships, and the ongoing maintenance of running your own infrastructure. Worth it for some workloads — not all.

The takeaway

Your data takes a journey, and the privacy story lives in the defaults and contracts more than in the technology. For non-sensitive prompts, the defaults are usually fine. For sensitive ones, read the ToS before sending anything — and consider self-hosting if the answer is unclear or unacceptable. "Where does my data go?" is always a fair question to ask.
